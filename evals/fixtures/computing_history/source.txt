A SHORT HISTORY OF COMPUTING
Background reading for HIST 2310: Technology and Society

Introduction

This reading traces the development of computing from mechanical calculating aids to networked personal devices. It is organised chronologically, but its main concern is not the sequence of machines so much as the ideas that made each step possible and the social conditions that shaped them. Throughout, it is worth remembering that the word computer originally referred to a person. For most of the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, computers were human beings, very often women, who carried out long calculations by hand for astronomical observatories, insurance companies, engineering firms and military projects. The machines that took over their work inherited their name.

Part 1. Before Electricity: Calculating Aids and Mechanical Calculators

The oldest calculating aids are counting boards and the abacus. An abacus represents numbers by the positions of beads on rods, each rod standing for a power of ten. In skilled hands it is remarkably fast for addition and subtraction, and in parts of East Asia it remained in everyday commercial use well into the second half of the twentieth century. The abacus does not calculate by itself, however; it is a memory aid that holds intermediate results while the operator does the arithmetic.

Logarithms, published by John Napier in 1614, transformed calculation by turning multiplication into addition. Because the logarithm of a product equals the sum of the logarithms of its factors, a person could multiply two large numbers by looking up their logarithms in a table, adding them, and looking up the result. Tables of logarithms became the indispensable tool of navigators, astronomers and engineers for the next three and a half centuries. The slide rule, invented by William Oughtred around 1622, put logarithmic scales on two sliding rulers so that multiplication could be carried out mechanically by adding lengths. Engineers relied on slide rules until pocket electronic calculators replaced them in the 1970s.

The first mechanical calculators appeared in the seventeenth century. Wilhelm Schickard designed a calculating clock in 1623, though it was apparently destroyed in a fire and was known only from letters. Blaise Pascal built an adding machine, later called the Pascaline, in 1642, reportedly to help his father, a tax official, with his arithmetic. The Pascaline used a train of geared wheels, one for each decimal digit, with a mechanism that carried a one to the next wheel whenever a wheel passed from nine to zero. Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz designed a machine in the 1670s that could also multiply, using a stepped drum, a cylinder with teeth of different lengths that remained the basis of mechanical calculators for two centuries. Leibniz is also remembered for describing binary arithmetic, in which every number is represented using only the digits zero and one.

Mechanical calculators did not become commercially important until the nineteenth century. The arithmometer of Thomas de Colmar, introduced in 1820 and manufactured in quantity from the 1850s, was the first calculator robust and reliable enough for daily office use. Later machines added keyboards, printing mechanisms and electric motors, and by the early twentieth century desk calculators were standard equipment in banks and engineering offices.

Part 2. Charles Babbage and Ada Lovelace

The decisive conceptual step towards the modern computer was taken by the English mathematician Charles Babbage. In the early nineteenth century, mathematical tables of logarithms, trigonometric functions and navigational quantities were computed by teams of human computers and were riddled with errors, introduced both in the calculation and in the typesetting. Babbage proposed to eliminate these errors with a machine that would calculate the tables and print them directly.

His first design, the Difference Engine, was based on the method of finite differences, which allows any polynomial to be tabulated using only repeated addition. Babbage began building the Difference Engine in the 1820s with government funding, but the project was abandoned in 1833 after disputes with his engineer, Joseph Clement, and after the government had spent a very large sum without receiving a working machine. A complete Difference Engine No. 2, built to Babbage's later designs, was finally constructed by the Science Museum in London and completed in 1991. It worked as Babbage had intended, showing that his designs had been sound and that the obstacles had been organisational and financial rather than technical. The Science Museum team also built the printing and stereotyping apparatus that Babbage had designed to go with the engine, and it was completed in 2002. The printer was intended to impress the results directly into soft material from which printing plates could be cast, so that no human typesetter would ever copy the numbers by hand and introduce new errors. The complete engine and printer together contain about 8,000 parts and weigh about five tonnes.

Babbage's difficulties were not only technical and financial. Precision engineering in the 1820s and 1830s could make parts to the required accuracy only slowly and at great expense, and Babbage repeatedly revised his designs while construction was under way, which made the project still more costly. Even so, the work on the Difference Engine contributed to the improvement of machine-tool practice in Britain, because the parts it demanded pushed the workshops that made them to new standards of precision.

While the Difference Engine was stalled, Babbage conceived a far more ambitious machine, the Analytical Engine. Unlike the Difference Engine, which could only carry out one kind of calculation, the Analytical Engine was to be a general-purpose machine controlled by a program. Its design separated a store, which held numbers, from a mill, which performed arithmetic on them, a division that anticipates the separation of memory and processor in modern computers. Instructions and data were to be supplied on punched cards of the kind used to control the patterns woven by the Jacquard loom, which had been introduced in France in 1804. The Analytical Engine could make decisions, choosing which instructions to carry out next depending on the result of a calculation, and it could repeat sequences of instructions. Babbage never built it, and only small parts were ever constructed.

The most important account of the Analytical Engine was written by Ada Lovelace, the daughter of the poet Lord Byron. In 1843 she translated into English an article about the engine by the Italian engineer Luigi Menabrea and added a set of notes of her own that were about three times as long as the original article. In the last of these notes, Note G, she described in detail how the engine could compute the Bernoulli numbers, a sequence important in number theory, and the procedure she set out is often described as the first published computer program. Lovelace also argued that the engine might act on things other than numbers, such as musical notes, if their relationships could be expressed in symbols, an insight that looked beyond calculation towards general symbol manipulation. At the same time she cautioned that the engine had no power to originate anything and could only do whatever it was ordered to perform.

Part 3. Punched Cards and the Data Processing Industry

The punched card became the basis of a large data-processing industry through the work of Herman Hollerith. For the United States census of 1880, the tabulation of results by hand took about eight years, and there were fears that the 1890 census would not be tabulated before the next one was due. Hollerith designed a system in which each person's answers were recorded as holes punched in a card. The cards were read by an electrical tabulating machine in which spring-loaded pins pressed down on each card; where there was a hole, a pin passed through into a cup of mercury and completed an electrical circuit that advanced a counter. Hollerith's machines processed the 1890 census far faster than the manual methods.

Hollerith's company, the Tabulating Machine Company, later merged with other firms, and in 1924 the combined company was renamed International Business Machines, or IBM. Under Thomas J. Watson, IBM became the dominant supplier of punched-card equipment, renting machines and selling enormous quantities of cards to governments and businesses. By the 1930s, punched-card machines could sort, collate, add and print, and large organisations maintained machine rooms in which cards passed through a sequence of specialised machines, each carrying out one step of the processing.

Punched-card methods were also used for scientific calculation. In the 1920s and 1930s the astronomer Leslie Comrie showed that commercial accounting machines could be adapted to compute astronomical tables, and Wallace Eckert at Columbia University used IBM machines for calculations in celestial mechanics. These applications showed that business machinery could be harnessed for science, a lesson that later shaped the early computer industry.

Part 4. Analogue Computers

Not all computing machines represented numbers as digits. Analogue computers represent quantities by continuously varying physical quantities, such as the rotation of a shaft, the length of a rod or an electrical voltage, and they solve problems by building a physical system that behaves according to the same equations as the problem.

Tide-predicting machines are among the earliest important examples. In 1872 William Thomson, later Lord Kelvin, built a machine that added together a number of sinusoidal motions, each representing one tidal component, using a system of pulleys and a wire to produce a curve predicting the height of the tide at a port. Tide predictors of this kind were used to prepare tide tables for the world's ports until the 1960s.

The most powerful mechanical analogue computer was the differential analyser, built by Vannevar Bush and his colleagues at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology and completed in 1931. It solved differential equations by connecting wheel-and-disc integrators through a system of shafts and gears, and setting it up for a new problem could take days of work with screwdrivers and spanners. Differential analysers were built in several countries and were used in the 1930s and 1940s for problems in physics, engineering and ballistics.

Electronic analogue computers, using operational amplifiers to add, multiply and integrate voltages, were widely used from the 1940s to the 1960s for simulating aircraft, guided missiles, chemical plants and electrical power systems. They were fast and gave an intuitive picture of the behaviour of a system, but their accuracy was limited to a few significant figures, and they were eventually displaced by digital computers as these became faster and cheaper.

One of the more unusual analogue computers was the MONIAC, built in 1949 by the New Zealand economist Bill Phillips while he was a student at the London School of Economics. It modelled the national economy of Britain with water flowing through a system of transparent tanks and pipes, in which the flows represented income, spending, taxation and saving, and valves could be adjusted to show the effect of changes in government policy. About a dozen copies were built and used for teaching economics at universities in several countries.

Part 5. The First Electronic Digital Computers

The years around the Second World War saw the construction of the first machines that could carry out long sequences of calculations automatically. Developments took place almost simultaneously in several countries, and questions of priority remain the subject of debate.

In Germany, Konrad Zuse built a series of machines in Berlin. His Z3, completed in 1941, was a program-controlled calculator built from about 2,600 telephone relays. It used binary floating-point arithmetic and read its program from punched film. The Z3 was destroyed in an air raid in 1943, and because of the war Zuse's work remained little known outside Germany for many years.

In the United States, John Vincent Atanasoff and his graduate student Clifford Berry built a machine at Iowa State College between 1939 and 1942 to solve systems of linear equations. The Atanasoff-Berry Computer used vacuum tubes for arithmetic and a rotating drum of capacitors for memory, and it is often credited as the first electronic digital calculating device, although it was not programmable. At Harvard University, Howard Aiken designed the Automatic Sequence Controlled Calculator, known as the Harvard Mark I, which was built by IBM and completed in 1944. It was an electromechanical machine more than fifteen metres long that read its instructions from punched paper tape. One of its first programmers was Grace Hopper, who later became a pioneer of programming languages.

In Britain, a series of electronic machines called Colossus was built at Bletchley Park to help break the Lorenz cipher used by the German high command. The first Colossus, designed by the Post Office engineer Tommy Flowers, was operational in early 1944. It contained about 1,600 vacuum tubes, and the improved Mark 2 version had about 2,400. Colossus was not a general-purpose computer, since it was designed for one class of cryptanalytic task, but it demonstrated that thousands of vacuum tubes could work together reliably if they were never switched off. The existence of Colossus remained secret until the 1970s, and most of the machines were dismantled after the war, which meant that its influence on later computer design was indirect.

The best known of the wartime machines was ENIAC, the Electronic Numerical Integrator and Computer, built at the Moore School of Electrical Engineering of the University of Pennsylvania by John Mauchly and J. Presper Eckert. It was funded by the United States Army to compute firing tables for artillery, which required huge numbers of trajectory calculations. ENIAC was completed in late 1945 and publicly announced in February 1946. It contained about 18,000 vacuum tubes, weighed about 27 tonnes, occupied a large room and consumed about 150 kilowatts of electricity. It could perform about 5,000 additions per second, roughly a thousand times faster than the electromechanical machines of the time.

ENIAC was programmed not by stored instructions but by setting switches and connecting its units with plugboard cables, and setting up a new problem could take days. The work of devising and setting up its programs was carried out largely by a group of women mathematicians originally hired as human computers, including Kathleen McNulty, Jean Jennings, Betty Snyder, Marlyn Wescoff, Frances Bilas and Ruth Lichterman. Their contribution was long overlooked; when ENIAC was publicly demonstrated, they were not introduced, and their role was recognised only decades later.

Part 6. The Stored-Program Concept

Even before ENIAC was finished, its designers recognised that its method of programming was a serious limitation. In discussions at the Moore School involving Eckert, Mauchly and the mathematician John von Neumann, a new design emerged in which the program would be stored in the same electronic memory as the data. In June 1945 von Neumann wrote the First Draft of a Report on the EDVAC, which described this design. The report circulated widely, and because only von Neumann's name appeared on it, the stored-program design became known as the von Neumann architecture, to the lasting resentment of Eckert and Mauchly.

In a stored-program computer, instructions are encoded as numbers and held in memory alongside the data they operate on. The processor fetches an instruction from memory, decodes it, executes it, and then fetches the next. Because instructions are just numbers in memory, a program can be loaded as quickly as data, and a program can even treat other programs as data, which makes compilers, loaders and operating systems possible. The theoretical foundation for such a universal machine had been laid in 1936 by Alan Turing, whose paper On Computable Numbers described an abstract machine, now called a Turing machine, that could carry out any computation that could be described by a definite procedure, and a universal machine that could imitate any other.

The first stored-program electronic computer to run a program was the Manchester Small-Scale Experimental Machine, nicknamed the Baby, built at the University of Manchester by Frederic Williams, Tom Kilburn and Geoff Tootill. It ran its first program on 21 June 1948. The Baby was built mainly to test a new form of memory, the Williams tube, which stored bits as charged spots on the screen of a cathode-ray tube. Its first program, written by Kilburn, found the highest proper factor of a number by trial division and ran for 52 minutes.

The first stored-program computer to provide a regular computing service was EDSAC, built at the University of Cambridge under the direction of Maurice Wilkes. EDSAC ran its first program on 6 May 1949 and was soon used by scientists throughout the university. Wilkes and his colleagues developed the idea of a library of subroutines, standard pieces of program that could be reused, and wrote the first textbook on programming. In 1951 the catering company J. Lyons and Co., which had sent observers to Cambridge, built its own computer based on EDSAC, called LEO, to handle its payroll and stock control, making it the first computer used for routine business data processing.

Part 7. Commercial Computers in the 1950s

After the war, Eckert and Mauchly left the University of Pennsylvania to form their own company, which built the UNIVAC I. The first UNIVAC was delivered to the United States Census Bureau in 1951. UNIVAC achieved public fame in 1952, when the television network CBS used it to predict the result of the presidential election. Early in the evening, with only a small fraction of the votes counted, it predicted a landslide victory for Dwight Eisenhower, which the network initially refused to believe because opinion polls had suggested a close race. The prediction proved accurate, and the word UNIVAC briefly became a synonym for computer.

IBM entered the electronic computer market somewhat cautiously but soon dominated it. Its 701, announced in 1952, was designed for scientific and defence work, and its 650, introduced in 1954, became the most widely used computer of the 1950s, with about 2,000 installed. The 650 used a rotating magnetic drum as its main memory and was attractive to universities partly because IBM offered large discounts to institutions that agreed to teach computing courses, which trained a generation of programmers familiar with IBM equipment.

Memory technology changed rapidly during the 1950s. Early machines used mercury delay lines, in which bits travelled as sound pulses through tubes of mercury, or Williams tubes, which were fast but unreliable. Magnetic core memory, developed in the early 1950s at MIT for the Whirlwind computer, stored each bit as the direction of magnetisation of a tiny ring of ferrite. Core memory was fast, reliable and retained its contents when the power was switched off, and it remained the dominant form of main memory until semiconductor memory replaced it in the 1970s. Magnetic tape and, from 1956, magnetic disk drives provided larger and cheaper secondary storage.

The university in which this course is taught acquired its first electronic computer in 1958, a second-hand machine purchased from an engineering firm and installed in the basement of the physics building. It was used mainly for numerical calculations by the physics and engineering departments, and students had to book time on it weeks in advance.

Part 8. Programming Languages

The first computers were programmed in machine code, the numeric instructions understood directly by the processor. Programming in machine code was slow and error-prone, and the next step was assembly language, in which each instruction is written as a short mnemonic and an assembler program translates the mnemonics into machine code. Assembly language was still tied closely to the design of a particular machine, and programs written for one computer could not run on another.

Grace Hopper was among the first to argue that computers could translate programs written in a language closer to English or mathematics into machine code. Her A-0 system of 1952 is often described as the first compiler, although it was closer to what would now be called a linker. She later led the development of FLOW-MATIC, a language for business data processing that used English words, and it strongly influenced COBOL, the Common Business-Oriented Language, which was designed by a committee in 1959 and became the standard language for business applications.

FORTRAN, short for Formula Translation, was developed at IBM by a team led by John Backus and released in 1957. It allowed scientists and engineers to write programs using algebraic formulas, and its compiler produced machine code nearly as efficient as code written by hand, which overcame the scepticism of programmers who believed that no compiler could match their own work. FORTRAN became the dominant language for scientific computing and, in updated versions, is still used for large numerical simulations.

Other influential languages followed. LISP, created by John McCarthy in 1958, introduced ideas such as recursion, automatic memory management and the treatment of programs as data, and it became the main language of artificial intelligence research for decades. ALGOL, designed by an international committee in 1958 and revised in 1960, introduced block structure and was described using a precise formal notation for its grammar, called Backus-Naur form. Although ALGOL was never widely used commercially, it influenced almost every later language. BASIC, created at Dartmouth College in 1964 by John Kemeny and Thomas Kurtz, was designed to be easy for students to learn and later became the standard language of early personal computers.

The C programming language was developed by Dennis Ritchie at Bell Laboratories between 1969 and 1973, alongside the Unix operating system. C combined the efficiency of assembly language with the convenience of a high-level language, and it became one of the most widely used programming languages in history.

Part 9. Transistors, Integrated Circuits and Moore's Law

Vacuum tubes were large, consumed a great deal of power, generated heat and failed frequently. The transistor, invented at Bell Laboratories in 1947 by John Bardeen, Walter Brattain and William Shockley, could perform the same switching and amplifying functions in a much smaller, cooler and more reliable form. The three inventors shared the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1956. The first computers built entirely from transistors appeared in the mid-1950s, and by the early 1960s transistorised machines had replaced vacuum-tube computers in most applications.

As computers grew more complex, the wiring together of thousands of individual transistors became a major problem in its own right. The solution was the integrated circuit, in which many transistors and the connections between them are made together on a single piece of semiconductor. Jack Kilby demonstrated an integrated circuit at Texas Instruments in 1958, and Robert Noyce at Fairchild Semiconductor independently developed a more practical version in 1959 using the planar process, in which the components are formed in a flat silicon surface and connected by a deposited layer of metal. Kilby received the Nobel Prize in Physics in 2000; Noyce had died in 1990.

In 1965 Gordon Moore, then at Fairchild Semiconductor, observed that the number of components that could be placed economically on an integrated circuit had been doubling every year, and he predicted that the trend would continue for at least a decade. In 1975 he revised the rate to a doubling about every two years. The prediction, known as Moore's law, held approximately for about half a century, and it became a self-fulfilling target for the semiconductor industry, which planned its research and investment around it. The steady growth in the number of transistors per chip drove continual improvements in the speed, cost and energy efficiency of computers.

In 1971 Intel, a company founded by Noyce and Moore in 1968, introduced the 4004, the first commercially available microprocessor, which placed the whole central processing unit of a simple computer on a single chip. The 4004 contained about 2,300 transistors and was designed for a Japanese calculator company, Busicom. It was followed by the 8-bit 8008 in 1972 and the 8080 in 1974, and the descendants of Intel's later 8086 processor of 1978 still power most desktop and laptop computers.

Part 10. Storage: From Magnetic Disks to Flash Memory

The amount of data that computers could hold grew even faster than their processing power. The first commercial hard disk drive, part of IBM's 305 RAMAC system of 1956, used fifty spinning metal platters, each 24 inches across, to store about five million characters, roughly five megabytes. It was the size of two large refrigerators and was leased for about 3,200 dollars a month. Because any record on the disk could be reached directly in less than a second, without reading through a tape from the beginning, the RAMAC made it practical for businesses to answer queries and update records as transactions happened rather than in overnight batches.

Over the following decades the density of data on magnetic disks increased by a factor of many millions. Smaller and cheaper drives brought disk storage to minicomputers and then to personal computers, which in their early years stored programs and data on removable floppy disks. The first floppy disks, introduced by IBM in the early 1970s, were 8 inches across; the 5.25-inch floppy became standard on personal computers in the late 1970s and early 1980s, and the 3.5-inch disk in a rigid plastic case, which held 1.44 megabytes in its most common form, became standard in the late 1980s.

Optical storage offered another route. The compact disc, developed jointly by Philips and Sony and introduced for music in 1982, was adapted for computer data as the CD-ROM, which could hold about 650 megabytes and was widely used in the 1990s to distribute software, encyclopaedias and games. The DVD, introduced in the mid-1990s, held several times as much.

Flash memory, a form of semiconductor memory that keeps its contents without power, was invented by Fujio Masuoka at Toshiba in the early 1980s. Flash memory has no moving parts, uses little power and survives being dropped, which made it ideal for cameras, music players and phones. As its price fell, flash memory began to replace magnetic disks in personal computers as well, in the form of solid-state drives, which are much faster than spinning disks because data can be read without waiting for a platter to rotate under a read head.

Part 11. Mainframes, Minicomputers and Time-Sharing

In 1964 IBM announced the System/360, a family of compatible computers covering a wide range of sizes and prices. Before the System/360, a customer who outgrew one computer usually had to rewrite its programs for a larger one. Because all members of the System/360 family shared the same instruction set, programs could be moved from a smaller to a larger machine without change. The project was one of the largest private investments of its time and a considerable gamble for IBM, but it succeeded spectacularly and established the mainframe architecture that IBM's machines still follow. The System/360 also made the 8-bit byte the standard unit of computer memory.

At the other end of the scale, companies such as Digital Equipment Corporation built minicomputers, smaller and much cheaper machines that a single laboratory or department could afford. DEC's PDP-8, introduced in 1965, sold for about 18,000 dollars and was small enough to stand on a laboratory bench. Minicomputers brought computing into laboratories, factories and small businesses and created a culture of hands-on interactive use quite different from the batch processing of the mainframe machine room.

In batch processing, users submitted their programs on decks of punched cards and collected the results hours or days later. Time-sharing allowed many users to work with a computer at once from terminals, each receiving a small slice of the processor's time in rapid rotation, so that each user had the illusion of a dedicated machine. The Compatible Time-Sharing System, demonstrated at MIT in 1961, was an early example, and the Dartmouth Time-Sharing System of 1964 made interactive computing available to most undergraduates at Dartmouth College.

Time-sharing created the need for operating systems that could protect users from each other and manage shared files. The ambitious Multics project, begun in 1964 by MIT, General Electric and Bell Laboratories, pioneered many of these ideas. When Bell Laboratories withdrew from Multics, Ken Thompson and Dennis Ritchie developed a much simpler system, Unix, beginning in 1969. Unix was rewritten in C in 1973, which made it unusually easy to move to new hardware, and its design ideas, such as a hierarchical file system and small programs that could be combined, influenced most later operating systems.

Part 12. The Software Crisis and Software Engineering

As computers became more powerful in the 1960s, the programs written for them grew enormously in size and complexity, and many large software projects ran years late, far over budget, or failed to work at all. The operating system for the IBM System/360, called OS/360, was a notorious example: it required thousands of programmers and was delivered late and with many errors. The term software crisis came to describe this widening gap between what hardware could do and what programmers could reliably build.

In 1968 the NATO Science Committee sponsored a conference in Garmisch, West Germany, under the deliberately provocative title Software Engineering. The organisers chose the name to suggest that software should be designed and built with the discipline of the established branches of engineering, with careful specification, systematic methods and measurable quality, rather than by improvisation. A second conference followed in Rome in 1969. The conferences did not solve the problems they described, but they gave the field its name and its agenda.

Frederick Brooks, who had managed the development of OS/360, drew lessons from that experience in his book The Mythical Man-Month, published in 1975. Its best-known claim, now called Brooks's law, is that adding programmers to a late software project makes it later, because new members must be trained and because the effort needed for communication grows rapidly as a team gets larger. Brooks also argued that a well-designed system should reflect a single coherent vision, which he called conceptual integrity.

Structured programming was one of the main responses to the crisis. In a famous letter of 1968 titled Go To Statement Considered Harmful, the Dutch computer scientist Edsger Dijkstra argued that the unrestricted use of jumps made programs impossible to reason about, and that programs should instead be built from a small number of well-understood control structures such as sequences, loops and conditional statements. Dijkstra, together with Tony Hoare and others, also developed methods for proving mathematically that a program meets its specification. Languages designed in the 1970s, such as Pascal, created by Niklaus Wirth in 1970, encouraged a structured style.

Later decades brought further approaches. Object-oriented programming, pioneered in the Simula language developed in Norway by Ole-Johan Dahl and Kristen Nygaard in the 1960s and popularised by Smalltalk at Xerox PARC in the 1970s and by C++ in the 1980s, organised programs around objects that combine data with the operations that act on it. In 2001 a group of developers published the Manifesto for Agile Software Development, which argued for short cycles of development, close collaboration with customers and willingness to change plans, in reaction to the heavy documentation and rigid planning of earlier methods.

Part 13. Databases

Businesses and governments adopted computers above all to keep records, and the management of large collections of data became one of the central problems of computing. Early systems stored records in files designed for a single application, so that the same information was often held in several places and became inconsistent when one copy was updated and another was not. Database management systems were developed in the 1960s to hold an organisation's data in one place and to provide a common way for many programs to use it.

The first database systems organised data either as hierarchies of records, as in IBM's Information Management System, developed in the 1960s partly to manage the vast parts lists of the Apollo space programme, or as networks of linked records, as in the Integrated Data Store designed by Charles Bachman at General Electric. In both approaches, programmers had to know how the records were linked in storage and navigate from one to another, which made programs hard to write and hard to change.

In 1970 Edgar F. Codd, a mathematician working at IBM's research laboratory in San Jose, published a paper proposing the relational model of data. In the relational model, data are represented as tables, called relations, made up of rows and columns, and users ask for the data they want by describing its properties rather than by specifying how to find it. The database system itself works out an efficient way to answer each request. Codd's proposal separated the logical organisation of data from its physical storage, which made databases far easier to use and to change.

IBM was initially slow to adopt Codd's ideas, partly because it had invested heavily in its hierarchical system. Its experimental System R project in the 1970s demonstrated that a relational database could perform well, and it produced the query language that became SQL, the Structured Query Language. A small company founded by Larry Ellison in 1977, later named Oracle, released one of the first commercial relational database systems in 1979, before IBM did. Relational databases became the standard technology for business data processing, and SQL remains one of the most widely used computer languages. Codd received the Turing Award, the highest honour in computer science, in 1981.

Part 14. Computer Graphics and Games

Computers were first used to produce images in the 1950s, when the displays of air defence and research computers showed radar tracks and simple diagrams. In 1963 Ivan Sutherland, a graduate student at MIT, created Sketchpad, a program that allowed a user to draw directly on the screen of a computer with a light pen and to manipulate the drawings as structured objects that could be copied, moved and resized. Sketchpad introduced many of the ideas of later graphical interfaces and computer-aided design, and Sutherland is often called the father of computer graphics.

Games played an important part in the history of computing, both as a test of programming skill and as a driver of hardware. Spacewar!, written in 1962 by Steve Russell and his colleagues at MIT for the PDP-1 minicomputer, was one of the first computer games, a battle between two spaceships around a star, and copies of it spread to research computers across the United States. The first commercially successful video arcade game, Pong, a simple simulation of table tennis, was released by Atari in 1972. Home video game consoles followed in the late 1970s, and in the 1980s games became one of the main reasons that families bought personal computers.

Computer animation developed from research laboratories into a major industry. The University of Utah, where Sutherland taught from 1968, trained many of the pioneers of three-dimensional graphics, who developed methods for shading curved surfaces, removing hidden lines and mapping images onto surfaces. The first entirely computer-animated feature film, Toy Story, was released by Pixar in 1995. The demand for realistic graphics in games and films drove the development of specialised graphics processors, which later found uses far beyond graphics.

Part 15. Personal Computers

By the mid-1970s, microprocessors had become cheap enough for individuals to buy a computer of their own. The Altair 8800, sold as a kit by the company MITS from 1975, is usually regarded as the machine that started the personal computer revolution. It had no keyboard or screen and was programmed with switches on its front panel, but it attracted thousands of hobbyists. Bill Gates and Paul Allen wrote a version of BASIC for the Altair and founded Microsoft to sell it.

In 1977 three fully assembled personal computers went on sale: the Apple II, designed by Steve Wozniak and marketed by Apple Computer, which Wozniak had founded with Steve Jobs; the Commodore PET; and the Tandy TRS-80. The Apple II became especially successful after the release in 1979 of VisiCalc, the first electronic spreadsheet, which gave businesses a compelling reason to buy a personal computer.

In 1981 IBM introduced the IBM Personal Computer, built largely from components bought from other companies and running an operating system, MS-DOS, licensed from Microsoft. Because the design was open and its parts were available to anyone, other manufacturers soon produced compatible machines, and the IBM PC and its clones became the dominant personal computer platform. Microsoft, which retained the right to license MS-DOS to other manufacturers, became the most powerful company in the industry.

The graphical user interface, with windows, icons, menus and a pointing device, was developed at the Xerox Palo Alto Research Center in the 1970s, building on the earlier work of Douglas Engelbart, who had demonstrated the computer mouse in 1968. Xerox's Alto computer of 1973 used such an interface, but Xerox did not market it successfully. Apple's Macintosh, introduced in 1984, brought the graphical interface to a mass market, and Microsoft's Windows, especially from version 3.0 in 1990, made it standard on IBM-compatible computers.

Part 16. Networks and the Internet

The Internet grew out of research funded by the Advanced Research Projects Agency of the United States Department of Defense. Its predecessor, the ARPANET, connected its first two computers, at the University of California, Los Angeles and the Stanford Research Institute, in October 1969. The first message was intended to be the word LOGIN, but the system crashed after the first two letters had been sent. The ARPANET used packet switching, an idea developed independently by Paul Baran in the United States and Donald Davies in Britain, in which messages are divided into small packets that travel separately through the network and are reassembled at their destination.

In the 1970s Vinton Cerf and Robert Kahn designed the Transmission Control Protocol and Internet Protocol, known together as TCP/IP, which allowed different networks to be joined into a single internetwork. The ARPANET switched to TCP/IP on 1 January 1983, a date often treated as the birth of the Internet. Electronic mail, invented by Ray Tomlinson in 1971, who chose the @ symbol to separate the user's name from the name of the host computer, quickly became the most popular use of the network.

The World Wide Web was proposed in 1989 by Tim Berners-Lee, a British computer scientist working at CERN, the European particle physics laboratory near Geneva. He developed the three basic elements of the Web, the HTML language for writing pages, the HTTP protocol for transferring them, and URLs for addressing them, together with the first web browser and server in 1990. CERN made the Web technology freely available in 1993, and in the same year the Mosaic browser, developed at the National Center for Supercomputing Applications in Illinois, made the Web easy to use with images displayed alongside text. The number of websites grew from a few hundred in 1993 to millions by the end of the decade.

Part 17. Search Engines and the Commercial Web

As the Web grew, finding information on it became a problem in its own right. Early efforts organised the Web by hand: Yahoo!, founded in 1994 by two Stanford graduate students, Jerry Yang and David Filo, began as a directory of websites arranged by topic. Automated search engines, which used programs called crawlers to download pages and build an index of the words they contained, soon proved better able to keep up with the rapidly growing number of pages. AltaVista, launched by Digital Equipment Corporation in 1995, was one of the most popular of these early engines.

Early search engines ranked pages mainly by how often and where the search words appeared on them, which made it easy for website owners to push their pages up the results by repeating popular words. In 1998 Larry Page and Sergey Brin, also graduate students at Stanford, founded Google around a different ranking method called PageRank. PageRank treated a link from one page to another as a vote for the second page and gave more weight to votes from pages that were themselves important. Because links were harder to manipulate than the words on a page, PageRank produced noticeably better results, and Google became the dominant search engine within a few years. Its business was built on advertising, displaying short text advertisements alongside the search results that matched the words a user had searched for.

The commercial use of the Internet had been restricted until the early 1990s, when the network that formed its backbone in the United States, run by the National Science Foundation, relaxed its rules, and the backbone itself was handed over to commercial providers in 1995. Online businesses multiplied. Amazon, founded by Jeff Bezos in 1994, began by selling books online, and eBay, founded in 1995, created a marketplace in which individuals auctioned goods to each other.

Investors poured money into new Internet companies in the late 1990s, often without regard to whether they made any profit, and share prices of technology companies rose to extraordinary levels. The boom ended in 2000, when share prices collapsed and many young companies went bankrupt, an episode known as the dot-com crash. The companies that survived, such as Amazon and Google, emerged in a stronger position, and in the following decade the Web became increasingly organised around a small number of very large platforms, including the social networking services that grew rapidly from the mid-2000s.

Part 18. Computer Security and Cryptography

Security became a central concern of computing as computers were shared and then connected. On a time-sharing system, one user's programs and files had to be protected from the mistakes and the curiosity of others, and the designers of Multics made security an explicit goal, introducing ideas such as rings of protection, in which the most trusted parts of the system run with privileges denied to ordinary programs. A study carried out for the United States Air Force in 1974 nevertheless showed that skilled testers could break into Multics, an early demonstration that security could not simply be added to a system after it was designed.

Networking multiplied the risks. On 2 November 1988 a program written by Robert Tappan Morris, a graduate student at Cornell University, began spreading across the Internet by exploiting weaknesses in common Unix programs. The Morris worm was apparently not intended to cause damage, but an error in its design caused it to copy itself repeatedly onto the same machines, and it slowed or disabled several thousand computers, a significant fraction of the Internet at the time. The incident led to the creation of the first computer emergency response team, at Carnegie Mellon University, to coordinate responses to such attacks, and Morris became the first person convicted under the United States Computer Fraud and Abuse Act of 1986.

Cryptography, the science of secret writing, was transformed by computing. Traditional ciphers required the sender and receiver to share a secret key in advance, which was a serious obstacle to secure communication between strangers over a network. In 1976 Whitfield Diffie and Martin Hellman published a method by which two parties could agree on a shared secret key over a public channel without ever transmitting the key itself. In 1977 Ron Rivest, Adi Shamir and Leonard Adleman at MIT described the RSA system, in which each user has a public key that anyone can use to encrypt a message and a private key, kept secret, that is needed to decrypt it. The security of RSA rests on the difficulty of factoring the product of two very large prime numbers. It later emerged that researchers at the British signals intelligence agency GCHQ had developed equivalent ideas a few years earlier, but their work had been kept secret.

Public-key cryptography made secure commerce on the Internet possible. When a web browser connects securely to a shop or a bank, it uses public-key methods to agree on a key with the server and to check that the server is genuine, and then uses faster conventional ciphers to protect the rest of the conversation. The Data Encryption Standard, adopted by the United States government in 1977, was the most widely used conventional cipher for two decades, until its 56-bit key became too short to resist attack by specially built machines, and it was replaced in 2001 by the Advanced Encryption Standard, based on a design by two Belgian cryptographers, Joan Daemen and Vincent Rijmen.

Part 19. Free Software and Open Source

In the early decades of computing, software was often shared freely. Computer manufacturers supplied programs with their machines, and users exchanged programs through user groups such as SHARE, founded in 1955 by users of IBM computers. This began to change in the late 1960s, when IBM, under pressure from an antitrust case brought by the United States government, decided in 1969 to price its software separately from its hardware. The decision helped to create an independent software industry, and over the following decade software was increasingly sold under licences that prohibited copying and kept the source code secret.

Richard Stallman, a programmer at the MIT Artificial Intelligence Laboratory, objected to this change. In 1983 he announced the GNU project, whose aim was to create a complete operating system compatible with Unix that would be free software, meaning that its users would be free to run, study, change and share it. In 1985 he founded the Free Software Foundation, and he wrote the GNU General Public License, which allows anyone to modify and redistribute a program on condition that the modified versions are also distributed under the same terms, an arrangement known as copyleft.

By the early 1990s the GNU project had produced many of the components of an operating system, including a compiler and an editor, but it still lacked a working kernel, the core of the system that manages the hardware. In 1991 Linus Torvalds, a student at the University of Helsinki, began writing a kernel as a personal project and released it on the Internet, inviting others to contribute. Combined with the GNU tools, the Linux kernel produced a complete free operating system. Thousands of volunteers and, later, many companies contributed to its development, and Linux now runs most of the world's servers and supercomputers, as well as the Android operating system used in most smartphones.

In 1998 a group of developers proposed the term open source as an alternative to free software, hoping that it would appeal more to businesses by emphasising the practical advantages of shared development rather than the ethical arguments that Stallman stressed. In the same year the company Netscape released the source code of its web browser, which later became the basis of the Firefox browser. Open-source programs such as the Apache web server, first released in 1995, became the foundations of much of the Internet, and the open development model, in which many contributors work together through the Internet, became standard practice across the software industry.

Part 20. Mobile and Ubiquitous Computing

Through the 1990s and 2000s computing spread from desks into pockets. Handheld organisers and mobile telephones gradually acquired more processing power, and the smartphone combined a telephone, a computer, a camera and a connection to the Internet in a single device. The introduction of Apple's iPhone in 2007, with its multi-touch screen, and of the Android operating system, released by Google in 2008, created a market in which billions of people carried a networked computer with them at all times.

Mobile devices depended on processors designed for low power consumption, many of them based on the ARM architecture, which originated in the 1980s at Acorn Computers in Cambridge, England. ARM processors use a reduced instruction set, a design approach that favours a small number of simple instructions that can be executed quickly and efficiently, and the company licenses its designs to other manufacturers rather than making chips itself.

At the same time, much of the computing that users rely on moved into large data centres operated by a few companies, an arrangement usually called cloud computing. Users store their files, run their applications and process their data on remote servers accessed through the Internet, paying for computing as a service rather than owning the machines. The combination of mobile devices and cloud services has made computing pervasive in daily life, while raising new questions about privacy, the concentration of economic power and the environmental cost of data centres.

Part 21. Artificial Intelligence

The idea that machines might think is older than the electronic computer, but it became a field of research only after computers were available to test it. In a paper of 1950 called Computing Machinery and Intelligence, Alan Turing proposed replacing the question of whether machines can think with a practical test, which he called the imitation game and which is now known as the Turing test. In the test, a human judge holds written conversations with a hidden machine and a hidden person, and the machine passes if the judge cannot reliably tell which is which.

The term artificial intelligence was coined by John McCarthy in the proposal for a summer research workshop held at Dartmouth College in 1956, which is usually regarded as the founding event of the field. The participants, who included Marvin Minsky, Claude Shannon, Allen Newell and Herbert Simon, were optimistic that machines could soon be made to use language, form concepts and solve problems. Newell and Simon presented the Logic Theorist, a program that proved theorems from Whitehead and Russell's Principia Mathematica, at about the same time, and in the following years programs were written that played checkers, solved algebra problems and answered questions in restricted subjects.

Progress turned out to be much slower than the pioneers had predicted. Problems such as understanding ordinary language or recognising objects in pictures, which people find easy, proved extremely hard for programs, and many methods that worked on small examples failed on realistic ones. Disappointment with the results led to sharp cuts in funding, first in the mid-1970s, after a critical report on British AI research by the mathematician James Lighthill in 1973, and again in the late 1980s. These periods of reduced funding and interest are known as AI winters.

In the late 1970s and 1980s, expert systems brought artificial intelligence into commercial use. An expert system captured the knowledge of human specialists as a large collection of if-then rules and used it to give advice in a narrow field. MYCIN, developed at Stanford University in the 1970s, recommended antibiotics for bacterial infections, and XCON, used by Digital Equipment Corporation from 1980, configured orders for its computer systems. Expert systems proved expensive to build and maintain, however, and difficult to extend beyond their narrow domains, and the market for them collapsed at the end of the 1980s.

A different approach, based on artificial neural networks loosely inspired by the brain, had been explored since the 1950s, when Frank Rosenblatt built the perceptron, a machine that learned to classify simple patterns. Neural networks learn from examples rather than following rules written by programmers. They were long held back by a lack of data and computing power, but in the 2000s and 2010s the availability of very large collections of data and of fast graphics processors made it possible to train networks with many layers, an approach called deep learning. In 2012 a deep neural network called AlexNet, developed at the University of Toronto, won an image recognition competition by a wide margin, and within a few years deep learning had transformed speech recognition, machine translation and image analysis. In 1997 IBM's Deep Blue had already defeated the world chess champion Garry Kasparov, and in 2016 the program AlphaGo, developed by DeepMind, defeated Lee Sedol, one of the strongest players of the board game Go, a result many researchers had expected to be a decade away.

Part 22. Computing and Society

The history of computing is also a history of labour and of who is recognised for technical work. As noted above, the first programmers of ENIAC were women, and in the 1950s and early 1960s programming was often regarded as clerical work suitable for women. As programming became better paid and more prestigious in the late 1960s and 1970s, the profession became increasingly male, and the share of women among computer science graduates in many countries fell from the mid-1980s onwards. Historians have argued that this shift resulted from changes in hiring practices, professional organisations and popular images of the programmer rather than from any change in the nature of the work.

Computing has repeatedly raised questions about privacy and surveillance. In the 1960s proposals for national data banks, which would have combined government records about individuals in a single computer system, provoked public opposition in several countries and led to the first data protection laws in the 1970s. The German state of Hesse passed what is usually regarded as the first data protection law in 1970, and Sweden passed the first national data protection law in 1973.

Automation has also been a persistent concern. Each new wave of computing technology has prompted fears that machines would replace human workers, from the punched-card machines that replaced clerks to the industrial robots and software that took over tasks in factories and offices. Historians generally find that computing has transformed the kinds of work people do more than it has reduced the total amount of work, but that its benefits and costs have been distributed very unevenly.

The unequal distribution of access to computers and to the Internet came to be called the digital divide in the 1990s. Within countries, access depended on income, age, education and whether a household lived in a city or in the countryside; between countries, the gap between rich and poor nations was even larger. The spread of cheap mobile phones in the 2000s and 2010s narrowed the gap considerably, because in many countries people gained access to the Internet through a phone without ever owning a personal computer, but large differences remain in the speed and cost of connections and in the skills needed to use them.

Accessibility for people with disabilities has been another long-standing concern. Screen readers, which convert the text on a screen into synthetic speech or into braille on a special display, made computers usable by blind people from the 1980s onwards, and the guidelines for accessible web pages published by the World Wide Web Consortium from 1999 encouraged designers to build pages that such tools could read.

Part 24. Computing in This University

The university's own computing history illustrates many of the themes of this reading. The university acquired its first electronic computer in 1962, when a new machine was installed in a purpose-built computing centre next to the mathematics department, and a small team of operators ran jobs submitted by staff from every faculty on decks of punched cards. A time-sharing service with terminals in several departments followed in the early 1970s, and the first networked personal computers appeared in student laboratories in the late 1980s.

Part 25. Supercomputers and Parallel Computing

Alongside the general-purpose computers used by businesses and universities, a separate line of machines was developed for the most demanding scientific calculations, such as weather forecasting, nuclear weapons design and the simulation of aircraft. These machines came to be called supercomputers. The first machines usually given that title were designed by Seymour Cray at Control Data Corporation in the 1960s. His CDC 6600 of 1964 was the fastest computer in the world for several years, achieving its speed through careful engineering and through the use of several independent functional units that could work at the same time. Cray later founded his own company, whose Cray-1 of 1976, with its distinctive C-shaped cabinet, became the most famous supercomputer of its era. The Cray-1 used vector processing, in which a single instruction operates on a whole list of numbers, which suited the large array calculations of scientific simulation.

From the late 1980s, supercomputer designers increasingly turned to massive parallelism, building machines from hundreds and then thousands of ordinary microprocessors working on parts of the same problem. Programming such machines required new methods for dividing a calculation among processors and exchanging results between them. Today the fastest supercomputers contain millions of processor cores, and many of them also use graphics processing units, chips originally designed to render images for computer games, because these can carry out very large numbers of simple arithmetic operations in parallel. The same graphics processors became the essential hardware for training the large neural networks of modern artificial intelligence.

The performance of supercomputers is measured in floating-point operations per second, or flops. The TOP500 list, published twice a year since 1993, ranks the world's most powerful supercomputers using a standard benchmark that solves a large system of linear equations. In 2022 the Frontier supercomputer at Oak Ridge National Laboratory in the United States became the first machine on the list to exceed one exaflop, a billion billion floating-point operations per second.

Conclusion

The development of computing was neither a straight line nor the work of a few heroic inventors. Mechanical calculators, punched-card tabulators, analogue machines and electronic digital computers developed side by side, and ideas were often discovered independently in several places. The decisive conceptual steps, such as the stored program, the compiler and the integrated circuit, each removed a bottleneck that had limited what earlier machines could do. Throughout this history, technical change was shaped by funding, by war, by the demands of business and government, and by the people, many of them unrecognised in their own time, who designed, built, programmed and operated the machines.
