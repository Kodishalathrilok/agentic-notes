INTRODUCTORY THERMODYNAMICS
Course Reader, Part II: Energy, Entropy and Engines

Chapter 1. Systems, States and the Language of Thermodynamics

Thermodynamics is the branch of physics that describes how energy is stored, transferred and converted in macroscopic systems. Unlike mechanics, which follows the trajectories of individual bodies, thermodynamics deliberately ignores the motion of individual molecules and works instead with a small number of bulk quantities such as pressure, volume, temperature and internal energy. The power of the subject comes from this restraint: its laws hold regardless of the microscopic details of the material, which is why the same reasoning applies to a steam turbine, a living cell and a star.

A thermodynamic system is the part of the universe chosen for study, and everything outside it is called the surroundings. The two are separated by a boundary, which may be real, like the walls of a cylinder, or imaginary, like a surface drawn around a parcel of air. Systems are classified by what can cross this boundary. An open system exchanges both matter and energy with its surroundings; a closed system exchanges energy but not matter; and an isolated system exchanges neither. A sealed, rigid, perfectly insulated flask is the textbook example of an isolated system, although no real container achieves perfect isolation.

The condition of a system at a given moment is described by its state variables. A state variable, also called a state function, depends only on the present condition of the system and not on how that condition was reached. Pressure, volume, temperature, internal energy and entropy are all state functions. Heat and work are not: they describe energy in transit during a process, and the amount of each depends on the path taken between two states. This distinction is the single most important idea in the first weeks of the course, and many errors in problem solving come from treating heat or work as if they were properties a system possesses.

State variables are further divided into extensive and intensive quantities. An extensive quantity scales with the size of the system: doubling the amount of material doubles its volume, its mass and its internal energy. An intensive quantity does not depend on size: temperature, pressure and density are the same for half a uniform sample as for the whole of it. The ratio of two extensive quantities is intensive, which is why specific volume, the volume per unit mass, behaves as an intensive property.

A system is in thermodynamic equilibrium when none of its state variables change with time and there are no net flows of matter or energy within it. Equilibrium has several parts. Thermal equilibrium means there is no net heat flow between parts of the system; mechanical equilibrium means there are no unbalanced forces, so the pressure is uniform; and chemical equilibrium means there is no net tendency for the composition to change. The state variables of classical thermodynamics are strictly defined only for equilibrium states, which is why the theory describes processes as sequences of equilibrium states.

A process that passes through a continuous sequence of equilibrium states is called quasi-static. A quasi-static process must be carried out infinitely slowly, so that the system has time to settle at every step. Real processes are never exactly quasi-static, but many are slow enough compared with the internal relaxation time of the system that the approximation is excellent. The compression of air in a bicycle pump, for example, is slow compared with the time sound takes to cross the pump barrel, so the pressure inside stays nearly uniform during each stroke.

Some processes are named for the quantity they hold fixed. An isothermal process takes place at constant temperature, an isobaric process at constant pressure, an isochoric process at constant volume, and an adiabatic process with no heat exchanged between the system and the surroundings. A process that returns a system to its initial state is called a cycle. Because state functions depend only on the state, the change of any state function around a complete cycle is zero, while the net heat and net work exchanged over the cycle generally are not zero. Every heat engine depends on that difference.

Temperature deserves special attention because it is the quantity that thermodynamics introduces that mechanics does not have. The zeroth law of thermodynamics states that if two systems are each in thermal equilibrium with a third system, they are in thermal equilibrium with each other. The law sounds obvious, but it is what makes thermometers possible: the third system can be a thermometer, and the law guarantees that two bodies giving the same thermometer reading will not exchange heat when placed in contact. The zeroth law was named after the first and second laws had already been numbered, because it was recognised as logically prior to both.

Practical temperature scales are built by choosing a thermometric property, such as the length of a mercury column or the resistance of a platinum wire, and assigning numbers to reproducible fixed points. The Celsius scale was historically defined using the freezing and boiling points of water at standard atmospheric pressure. The Kelvin scale is an absolute scale whose zero is absolute zero, the lowest temperature physically possible. A temperature difference of one kelvin is equal to a temperature difference of one degree Celsius, and a temperature in kelvins is obtained by adding 273.15 to the temperature in degrees Celsius. In thermodynamic formulas the temperature must always be expressed on an absolute scale; using degrees Celsius in the ideal gas law or in an efficiency formula is one of the most common examination mistakes.

The ideal gas is the simplest model system in thermodynamics. Its equation of state is pV = nRT, where p is the pressure, V is the volume, n is the amount of gas in moles, T is the absolute temperature, and R is the molar gas constant, approximately 8.314 J/(mol·K). An ideal gas is one whose molecules occupy negligible volume and exert no forces on each other except during collisions. Real gases approach ideal behaviour at low pressure and high temperature, where the molecules are far apart and move quickly. At high pressure or near condensation the ideal gas law can be badly wrong, and more elaborate equations of state, such as the van der Waals equation, are needed.

Pressure is force per unit area, and its SI unit is the pascal, equal to one newton per square metre. The pascal is a small unit, so pressures in engineering are often quoted in kilopascals, megapascals or bar, where one bar is exactly 100 kilopascals. Standard atmospheric pressure is defined as 101.325 kilopascals. Pressure gauges usually read gauge pressure, the difference between the absolute pressure and the local atmospheric pressure, so a tyre gauge reading of 200 kilopascals corresponds to an absolute pressure of about 300 kilopascals. Thermodynamic formulas always require the absolute pressure, and confusing the two is a frequent source of error in problems involving gas cylinders and tyres.

The amount of a substance is measured in moles. One mole contains the Avogadro number of particles, about 6.022 times 10 to the power 23. The molar mass of a substance is the mass of one mole, so 18 grams of water or 28 grams of nitrogen gas each contain about one mole of molecules. The Boltzmann constant k equals the molar gas constant R divided by the Avogadro number, and it plays the role of a gas constant per molecule rather than per mole. Formulas written for individual molecules use k, while formulas written for moles of gas use R.

The van der Waals equation modifies the ideal gas law with two constants. The constant a accounts for the attraction between molecules, which reduces the pressure the gas exerts on the walls, and the constant b accounts for the finite volume of the molecules themselves, which reduces the volume available for their motion. Although the van der Waals equation is not accurate enough for engineering design, it is valuable because it predicts the existence of a critical point, above which the distinction between liquid and gas disappears.

Chapter 2. Work, Heat and Internal Energy

Energy can cross the boundary of a closed system in two ways: as work and as heat. Work is energy transferred by a macroscopic, organised motion, such as a piston moving against a force or a shaft turning a paddle. Heat is energy transferred because of a temperature difference between the system and its surroundings. Both are measured in joules. In this reader we adopt the sign convention used in most physics textbooks: Q is the heat added to the system, and W is the work done by the system on its surroundings. Chemistry texts often define W as the work done on the system instead, and students moving between courses must check the convention before comparing formulas.

The most common form of work in thermodynamics is the boundary work done when a system changes volume against an external pressure. For a quasi-static process the work done by the system is the integral of p dV from the initial to the final volume. On a pressure-volume diagram this integral is the area under the curve that represents the process. Because different paths between the same two states enclose different areas, the work depends on the path, which is a graphical demonstration that work is not a state function.

For an isobaric process the boundary work is simply p multiplied by the change in volume. For an isochoric process no boundary work is done at all, because the volume does not change. For the isothermal expansion of an ideal gas from volume V1 to volume V2, the work done by the gas is nRT ln(V2/V1). This expression is positive for an expansion and negative for a compression, consistent with the sign convention that W is the work done by the system.

Heat transfer occurs by three mechanisms. Conduction is the transfer of energy through a material by collisions between neighbouring particles, without any bulk motion of the material. Convection is transfer by the bulk motion of a fluid, as when warm air rises from a radiator and is replaced by cooler air. Radiation is transfer by electromagnetic waves and is the only mechanism that works through a vacuum, which is how energy from the Sun reaches the Earth. In most practical situations more than one mechanism operates at once, and engineers must estimate the contribution of each.

The rate of conduction through a flat slab is described by Fourier's law: the heat flow per unit time is proportional to the area of the slab and to the temperature difference across it, and inversely proportional to its thickness. The constant of proportionality is the thermal conductivity of the material. Metals such as copper have high thermal conductivities, while still air, wool and expanded polystyrene have low ones. Most good thermal insulators work by trapping small pockets of air so that convection cannot develop.

When heat is conducted through several layers in series, such as the plaster, brick, insulation and cladding of a house wall, each layer can be described by a thermal resistance, equal to its thickness divided by its thermal conductivity and its area. The total resistance of layers in series is the sum of the individual resistances, just as for electrical resistors in series, and the heat flow equals the overall temperature difference divided by the total resistance. Building regulations specify the maximum allowed heat loss through walls, roofs and windows in terms of a quantity called the U-value, the heat flow per unit area per kelvin of temperature difference. A lower U-value means better insulation; a modern triple-glazed window has a U-value several times lower than an old single-glazed one.

Convective heat transfer between a solid surface and a moving fluid is described by Newton's law of cooling, which states that the rate of heat transfer is proportional to the surface area and to the temperature difference between the surface and the fluid far from it. The constant of proportionality, called the convective heat transfer coefficient, is not a property of the material alone. It depends on the fluid, on its speed, and on the shape of the surface. Natural convection, driven only by buoyancy, gives small coefficients; forced convection, where a fan or pump drives the fluid, gives much larger ones; and boiling and condensation give the largest of all, which is why steam is such an effective medium for heating.

Every surface emits thermal radiation at a rate that depends on its absolute temperature. A perfect emitter, called a black body, radiates energy per unit area at a rate proportional to the fourth power of its absolute temperature, a result known as the Stefan-Boltzmann law. The constant of proportionality, the Stefan-Boltzmann constant, is about 5.67 times 10 to the power minus 8 watts per square metre per kelvin to the fourth. Real surfaces radiate less than a black body, and the ratio of their emission to that of a black body at the same temperature is called the emissivity, a number between zero and one. Polished metals have low emissivities, which is why the inner walls of vacuum flasks are silvered, while matt black paint has an emissivity close to one.

The strong temperature dependence of radiation means that it becomes the dominant mode of heat transfer at high temperatures. Inside a furnace or a combustion chamber, most of the heat reaching the walls arrives as radiation. At room temperature radiation is still significant: a person standing indoors loses a substantial part of their body heat by radiation to the cooler walls, which is why a room with cold walls feels chilly even when the air is warm.

The wavelength at which a black body radiates most strongly is inversely proportional to its absolute temperature, a relationship known as Wien's displacement law. The Sun, with a surface temperature of about 5800 K, radiates most strongly in the visible part of the spectrum, while the Earth, at about 288 K, radiates mainly in the infrared. Greenhouse gases such as carbon dioxide and water vapour are largely transparent to the incoming visible sunlight but absorb part of the outgoing infrared radiation, which raises the surface temperature of the Earth above the value it would have without an atmosphere.

The heat capacity of an object is the heat required to raise its temperature by one kelvin. Specific heat capacity is the heat capacity per unit mass, and molar heat capacity is the heat capacity per mole. Water has an unusually high specific heat capacity of about 4186 J/(kg·K), which is why oceans moderate coastal climates and why water is widely used as a coolant. For gases one must specify the conditions under which heat is added, because a gas heated at constant pressure expands and does work, so it needs more heat for the same temperature rise than a gas heated at constant volume. The molar heat capacity at constant pressure, Cp, therefore exceeds the molar heat capacity at constant volume, Cv. For an ideal gas the difference Cp minus Cv is exactly equal to R.

The ratio of the two heat capacities, Cp divided by Cv, is usually written with the Greek letter gamma. For a monatomic ideal gas such as helium or argon, gamma is 5/3, or about 1.67. For a diatomic gas such as nitrogen or oxygen near room temperature, gamma is about 1.4. The value of gamma controls how steeply the pressure rises in an adiabatic compression and enters directly into the formula for the speed of sound in a gas.

Calorimetry is the experimental measurement of heat. In a simple coffee-cup calorimeter a reaction or a hot object is placed in a known mass of water inside an insulated container, and the temperature change of the water is recorded. If the calorimeter is well insulated, the heat released by the sample equals the heat absorbed by the water and the container. The container itself absorbs some heat, so an accurate measurement requires its heat capacity to be known. In the department's teaching laboratory, the aluminium calorimeter used in Experiment 3 has a heat capacity of 85 J/K, and this value must be included in every energy balance for that experiment. Neglecting the container is the largest single source of error in student calorimetry reports.

Internal energy, written U, is the total energy contained in a system because of the motions and interactions of its particles. It includes the kinetic energy of molecular translation, rotation and vibration, and the potential energy of the forces between molecules. It excludes the kinetic energy of the system as a whole and its potential energy in external fields, which are treated by mechanics. Internal energy is a state function, so the change in U between two states is the same for every process that connects them. For an ideal gas the internal energy depends only on the temperature, because there are no intermolecular forces whose potential energy could depend on the volume.

The equipartition theorem gives a simple estimate of the internal energy of an ideal gas. It states that, in thermal equilibrium, each quadratic degree of freedom of a molecule carries an average energy of one half of kT, where k is the Boltzmann constant. A monatomic gas has three translational degrees of freedom, so its molar internal energy is 3/2 RT and its molar heat capacity at constant volume is 3/2 R. A diatomic molecule at room temperature also has two rotational degrees of freedom, giving 5/2 R for Cv. Vibrational degrees of freedom become active only at higher temperatures, a fact that classical physics could not explain and that was later accounted for by quantum mechanics.

Chapter 3. The First Law of Thermodynamics

The first law of thermodynamics is the law of conservation of energy applied to thermodynamic systems. In the sign convention of this reader, it states that the change in internal energy of a closed system equals the heat added to the system minus the work done by the system: delta U = Q minus W. The law asserts that energy is neither created nor destroyed, only transferred or converted from one form to another. It rules out any machine that produces work continuously without an energy input, which is called a perpetual motion machine of the first kind.

The historical path to the first law ran through the recognition that heat is a form of energy rather than a fluid. Until the nineteenth century many scientists held the caloric theory, in which heat was an invisible, weightless fluid called caloric that flowed from hot bodies to cold ones and was conserved. Benjamin Thompson, Count Rumford, observed in the 1790s that boring cannon barrels produced apparently unlimited heat from mechanical work, which was difficult to reconcile with a conserved fluid. James Prescott Joule later measured the mechanical equivalent of heat by using falling weights to turn a paddle wheel in an insulated container of water and recording the temperature rise. His experiments showed that a fixed amount of work always produced the same amount of heating, which established the equivalence of work and heat.

Applied to particular processes, the first law gives useful special cases. In an isochoric process no boundary work is done, so all the heat added goes into internal energy and delta U equals Q. In an adiabatic process no heat is exchanged, so the work done by the system comes entirely from its internal energy and delta U equals minus W; an adiabatic expansion therefore cools a gas, and an adiabatic compression heats it. In an isothermal process of an ideal gas the internal energy does not change, because it depends only on temperature, so the heat added exactly equals the work done by the gas. In a complete cycle the internal energy returns to its initial value, so the net heat added over the cycle equals the net work done.

Adiabatic processes of an ideal gas obey the relation p V to the power gamma equals a constant, provided the process is quasi-static. Because gamma is greater than one, an adiabatic curve on a pressure-volume diagram is steeper than an isothermal curve through the same point. This is why diesel engines can ignite their fuel without a spark: the rapid compression of air is nearly adiabatic, and the temperature rises high enough to ignite the injected fuel spontaneously. The same principle explains why the air pumped into a bicycle tyre warms the pump barrel.

For open systems with steady flow, such as turbines, compressors and nozzles, the first law is usually written in terms of enthalpy. Enthalpy, H, is defined as U plus pV. For a process at constant pressure the heat added equals the change in enthalpy, which is why chemists report the heats of reactions carried out in open vessels as enthalpy changes. In a steady-flow device the pV term accounts for the work needed to push fluid into and out of the device, known as flow work. Steam tables used by engineers list the specific enthalpy of water and steam at many combinations of temperature and pressure.

A throttling process, in which a gas or liquid is forced through a valve or porous plug from high pressure to low pressure without exchanging heat or doing shaft work, keeps the enthalpy constant. For an ideal gas a throttling process leaves the temperature unchanged, because the enthalpy of an ideal gas depends only on temperature. For real gases the temperature generally changes, an effect called the Joule-Thomson effect. Most gases cool when throttled at room temperature, and this cooling is the basis of many refrigeration and gas liquefaction processes. Hydrogen and helium warm when throttled at room temperature and must first be precooled below their inversion temperatures before throttling can cool them.

Chapter 4. Heat Engines and the Second Law

A heat engine is a device that operates in a cycle, takes in heat from a hot reservoir, converts part of it into work, and rejects the rest as heat to a cold reservoir. A reservoir is an idealised body so large that its temperature does not change when heat is added or removed. The thermal efficiency of a heat engine is defined as the net work output divided by the heat taken from the hot reservoir. Because the internal energy returns to its initial value after each cycle, the work output equals the heat absorbed minus the heat rejected, so the efficiency can also be written as one minus the ratio of the heat rejected to the heat absorbed.

The first law alone would allow an engine to convert all of the heat it absorbs into work. Experience shows that this never happens, and the second law of thermodynamics makes that experience a principle. The Kelvin-Planck statement of the second law says that it is impossible for any device operating in a cycle to receive heat from a single reservoir and produce an equal amount of work. In other words, every heat engine must reject some heat to a colder reservoir, and no heat engine can have an efficiency of 100 percent. A machine that violated this statement would be a perpetual motion machine of the second kind.

The Clausius statement of the second law says that it is impossible for any device operating in a cycle to transfer heat from a colder body to a hotter body without some other effect, such as the input of work. Heat flows spontaneously from hot to cold, and making it flow the other way always has a cost. The Kelvin-Planck and Clausius statements look different, but they are logically equivalent: a device violating either one could be combined with an ordinary engine or refrigerator to build a device violating the other.

A refrigerator is a heat engine run in reverse. It uses work to extract heat from a cold space and reject it to a warmer environment. Its performance is measured by the coefficient of performance, defined as the heat extracted from the cold space divided by the work input. Unlike an efficiency, a coefficient of performance is usually greater than one; a typical household refrigerator removes two to four joules of heat from its interior for every joule of electrical work. A heat pump uses the same cycle to deliver heat to a warm space, and its coefficient of performance is defined as the heat delivered to the warm space divided by the work input.

A reversible process is one that can be reversed so that both the system and its surroundings return exactly to their original states. Reversible processes are idealisations. Real processes are irreversible because of effects such as friction, unrestrained expansion, mixing of different substances, and heat transfer across a finite temperature difference. Each of these effects dissipates the capacity to do work. Engineers improve real machines largely by reducing these sources of irreversibility, for example by lubricating moving parts and by using heat exchangers with large surface areas so that heat can flow across smaller temperature differences.

The Carnot cycle, proposed by the French engineer Sadi Carnot in 1824, is a reversible cycle consisting of four steps: an isothermal expansion at the hot reservoir temperature, an adiabatic expansion that cools the working substance to the cold reservoir temperature, an isothermal compression at the cold reservoir temperature, and an adiabatic compression that returns the working substance to its initial state. Carnot developed his analysis while the caloric theory was still widely accepted, yet his central conclusions survived the later recognition that heat is a form of energy.

The Carnot theorem states that no engine operating between two given reservoirs can be more efficient than a reversible engine operating between the same reservoirs, and that all reversible engines operating between the same two reservoirs have the same efficiency. The efficiency of a Carnot engine depends only on the absolute temperatures of the reservoirs and equals one minus the ratio of the cold reservoir temperature to the hot reservoir temperature. For an engine working between 600 K and 300 K, the Carnot efficiency is therefore 50 percent. Real engines achieve substantially less than the Carnot limit because of irreversibilities, and the gap between actual and Carnot efficiency is a useful measure of how much improvement is possible.

The Carnot efficiency formula has important practical consequences. To increase the maximum possible efficiency of an engine, one must either raise the temperature at which heat is supplied or lower the temperature at which it is rejected. The cold reservoir is usually the atmosphere or a river, so its temperature cannot be lowered much. Engine designers therefore push the hot temperature as high as materials allow, which is why turbine blades are made of special nickel alloys and are often cooled internally by air flowing through small channels.

The second law also leads to the definition of the thermodynamic temperature scale. Because the efficiency of a reversible engine depends only on the temperatures of its reservoirs, the ratio of the heats exchanged by a reversible engine can be used to define the ratio of the two temperatures, independently of the properties of any particular substance. Kelvin proposed this definition, and the resulting thermodynamic temperature scale coincides with the ideal gas temperature scale wherever the ideal gas thermometer can be used.

Chapter 5. Entropy

Entropy is the state function that makes the second law quantitative. For a reversible process, the change in entropy of a system is defined as the integral of the heat added divided by the absolute temperature at which it is added: dS equals dQ reversible divided by T. Entropy has units of joules per kelvin. Because entropy is a state function, the change in entropy between two states is the same for every path connecting them, even though it must be calculated along a reversible path. To find the entropy change in an irreversible process, one imagines any convenient reversible path between the same initial and final states and calculates along that path.

Clausius summarised the second law in terms of entropy: the entropy of an isolated system never decreases. It stays constant in reversible processes and increases in irreversible ones. Because the universe as a whole can be treated as an isolated system, every real process increases the total entropy of the universe, although the entropy of a particular part of it may decrease. A refrigerator lowers the entropy of its contents, for example, but it raises the entropy of the kitchen by a larger amount.

Several simple entropy changes follow directly from the definition. When an ideal gas expands isothermally and reversibly from volume V1 to V2, its entropy increases by nR ln(V2/V1). When a substance of constant heat capacity C is heated from T1 to T2, its entropy increases by C ln(T2/T1). When a substance melts at its melting temperature, its entropy increases by the latent heat of fusion divided by the melting temperature. When an ideal gas undergoes a free expansion into a vacuum, no heat is exchanged and no work is done, but the entropy still increases by nR ln(V2/V1), because entropy is a state function and the final state is the same as in the reversible isothermal expansion.

The free expansion example shows clearly that entropy is not the same as heat. In a free expansion no heat flows at all, yet the entropy increases, because the process is irreversible. The gas will never spontaneously return to the smaller volume, even though doing so would not violate the conservation of energy. The second law, rather than the first, explains why.

When heat Q flows from a hot body at temperature Th to a cold body at temperature Tc, the hot body loses entropy Q divided by Th and the cold body gains entropy Q divided by Tc. Because Tc is smaller than Th, the cold body gains more entropy than the hot body loses, and the total entropy increases. The calculation shows that heat flow across a finite temperature difference is always irreversible, and that the irreversibility grows with the size of the temperature difference.

Entropy also has a statistical interpretation, developed by Ludwig Boltzmann. A macroscopic state, described by quantities such as energy and volume, can be realised by an enormous number of microscopic arrangements of the molecules, called microstates. Boltzmann proposed that the entropy is proportional to the logarithm of the number of microstates: S equals k ln W, where W is the number of microstates compatible with the macroscopic state. This formula is engraved on Boltzmann's tombstone in Vienna. In the statistical picture, entropy increases because systems wander through their microstates and are overwhelmingly likely to be found in macrostates that correspond to the largest number of microstates.

The statistical interpretation explains why the second law is a law of probability rather than of strict necessity. For a system of a few molecules, a temporary decrease in entropy is quite likely. For a macroscopic system containing about 10 to the power 23 molecules, the probability of a noticeable spontaneous decrease is so small that it would not be expected to occur even once in the age of the universe. This is why the second law can be treated as absolute for everyday purposes.

The temperature-entropy diagram is a useful companion to the pressure-volume diagram. On a T-S diagram the area under a reversible process curve equals the heat added to the system, just as the area under a p-V curve equals the work done. A Carnot cycle appears on a T-S diagram as a rectangle, with two horizontal lines for the isothermal steps and two vertical lines for the reversible adiabatic steps, which are isentropic. The area of the rectangle equals the net work output of the cycle.

Chapter 6. Thermodynamic Potentials and Equilibrium

The internal energy is the natural potential for an isolated system, but most laboratory processes take place under other conditions, such as constant temperature and pressure. For these conditions thermodynamics defines additional state functions, called thermodynamic potentials, each of which reaches a minimum at equilibrium under the corresponding constraints.

The Helmholtz free energy, F, is defined as U minus TS. For a system held at constant temperature and volume, a spontaneous process lowers the Helmholtz free energy, and equilibrium is reached when F is at a minimum. The decrease in F during an isothermal process equals the maximum work that the system can do in that process, which is why the Helmholtz free energy is sometimes called the work function.

The Gibbs free energy, G, is defined as H minus TS. For a system held at constant temperature and pressure, a spontaneous process lowers the Gibbs free energy, and equilibrium is reached when G is at a minimum. Because most chemical reactions take place at roughly constant temperature and pressure, the Gibbs free energy is the most widely used potential in chemistry. A reaction with a negative change in Gibbs free energy can proceed spontaneously, although the Gibbs free energy says nothing about how fast it will proceed; a mixture of hydrogen and oxygen at room temperature has a large negative Gibbs free energy of reaction yet remains unreacted for years unless a spark starts the reaction.

The change in Gibbs free energy combines two tendencies. The enthalpy term favours processes that release heat, and the entropy term, multiplied by the temperature, favours processes that increase disorder. At low temperatures the enthalpy term usually dominates, and at high temperatures the entropy term becomes more important. This competition explains why ice melts above 0 degrees Celsius at atmospheric pressure: melting absorbs heat, which is unfavourable, but it increases the entropy, and above the melting point the entropy term wins.

Phase equilibria are a central application of the Gibbs free energy. Two phases of a substance, such as liquid water and water vapour, can coexist in equilibrium only when their molar Gibbs free energies are equal. A phase diagram shows, as a function of temperature and pressure, which phase has the lowest Gibbs free energy. The lines on the diagram are the coexistence curves, where two phases have equal Gibbs free energy. The point where the solid, liquid and gas coexistence curves meet is the triple point, where all three phases coexist. For water the triple point lies at 273.16 K and a pressure of about 611 pascals.

The liquid-gas coexistence curve ends at the critical point. Above the critical temperature, no amount of pressure can liquefy the gas, and the substance is called a supercritical fluid. For water the critical temperature is about 647 K and the critical pressure is about 22.1 megapascals. Supercritical carbon dioxide is used industrially as a solvent, for example to decaffeinate coffee, because it can dissolve organic compounds and then be removed completely by lowering the pressure.

The slope of a coexistence curve is related to the latent heat and the volume change of the transition. Water is unusual in that its solid phase is less dense than its liquid phase, so the solid-liquid coexistence curve has a negative slope: increasing the pressure lowers the melting point of ice slightly. Most substances behave the other way, with a solid that is denser than the liquid and a melting point that rises with pressure. The expansion of water on freezing is responsible for burst pipes in winter and for the weathering of rocks by frost.

The chemical potential of a component of a mixture is the partial derivative of the Gibbs free energy with respect to the amount of that component, at constant temperature, pressure and amounts of the other components. Matter tends to flow from regions of high chemical potential to regions of low chemical potential, just as heat flows from high to low temperature. At equilibrium, the chemical potential of each component is the same in every phase in which it is present. Osmosis, the flow of solvent through a membrane that blocks the solute, is a direct consequence of differences in chemical potential.

The Gibbs free energy also governs chemical equilibrium. For a reaction in a mixture of gases, the standard Gibbs free energy change of the reaction is related to the equilibrium constant K by the expression delta G standard equals minus RT ln K. A large negative standard Gibbs free energy change corresponds to an equilibrium constant much greater than one, meaning that at equilibrium the products greatly outnumber the reactants. A positive standard Gibbs free energy change corresponds to an equilibrium constant smaller than one, so that little product is formed. The equilibrium constant depends only on temperature, not on the initial amounts of the substances.

Le Chatelier's principle summarises how an equilibrium responds to a disturbance: the system shifts in the direction that partly counteracts the change. Adding more of a reactant shifts the equilibrium towards the products; raising the pressure of a gas-phase reaction shifts it towards the side with fewer gas molecules; and raising the temperature shifts it in the direction that absorbs heat. The industrial synthesis of ammonia from nitrogen and hydrogen illustrates the trade-offs involved. The reaction releases heat and reduces the number of gas molecules, so low temperature and high pressure favour a high yield, but at low temperature the reaction is impractically slow. The Haber-Bosch process therefore operates at a compromise temperature of roughly 400 to 450 degrees Celsius, a pressure of about 200 atmospheres, and with an iron catalyst to increase the rate.

The temperature dependence of the equilibrium constant is described by the van 't Hoff equation, which relates the rate of change of ln K with temperature to the standard enthalpy change of the reaction. For an exothermic reaction the equilibrium constant falls as the temperature rises, and for an endothermic reaction it increases. Measuring the equilibrium constant at several temperatures and plotting ln K against the reciprocal of the temperature gives a straight line whose slope yields the standard enthalpy of reaction, a method widely used in physical chemistry laboratories.

Electrochemical cells provide another route to the Gibbs free energy. In a galvanic cell a spontaneous chemical reaction drives an electric current through an external circuit, and the maximum electrical work the cell can deliver equals the decrease in Gibbs free energy of the reaction. The cell voltage measured when no current flows is therefore a direct measurement of the Gibbs free energy change per unit of charge transferred. Batteries and fuel cells are practical applications of this principle. A hydrogen fuel cell converts the Gibbs free energy of the reaction of hydrogen with oxygen directly into electricity, and because it is not a heat engine, its efficiency is not limited by the Carnot formula, although other losses keep practical fuel cell efficiencies well below their theoretical maximum.

Chapter 7. Practical Power and Refrigeration Cycles

The Carnot cycle sets the upper limit on efficiency, but it is not a practical design. Isothermal heat transfer is slow, and the large volume changes required for the isothermal steps make Carnot engines bulky. Practical engines use other cycles that approximate the ideal more or less closely while delivering useful power at a reasonable size and cost.

The Rankine cycle is the basic cycle of steam power plants. Water is pumped to high pressure, heated in a boiler until it becomes superheated steam, expanded through a turbine that drives an electrical generator, and condensed back to liquid in a condenser cooled by river water, sea water or cooling towers. Because the working fluid is pumped while it is a liquid, the pump work is very small compared with the turbine work, which is a major advantage of the Rankine cycle. The efficiency of the cycle is improved by raising the boiler pressure and temperature, by superheating the steam, by reheating the steam between turbine stages, and by regeneration, in which steam extracted from the turbine is used to preheat the feedwater.

A large modern coal-fired power station operating on a supercritical Rankine cycle reaches a thermal efficiency of roughly 45 percent, while older subcritical plants typically reach 33 to 38 percent. Nuclear power plants operate at lower steam temperatures because of limits on the fuel and reactor materials, and their thermal efficiencies are usually around 33 percent.

The Brayton cycle is the ideal cycle for gas turbines, such as those in jet engines and in gas-fired power stations. Air is compressed, fuel is burned at constant pressure, and the hot gases expand through a turbine. Part of the turbine work drives the compressor, and the rest is available as net output. The efficiency of the ideal Brayton cycle depends on the pressure ratio of the compressor: the higher the pressure ratio, the higher the efficiency, up to limits set by the maximum temperature the turbine materials can tolerate.

In a combined-cycle power plant, the hot exhaust from a gas turbine, which is still at several hundred degrees Celsius, is used to raise steam for a Rankine cycle. The combination recovers heat that a simple gas turbine would waste, and combined-cycle plants reach thermal efficiencies above 60 percent, the highest of any large-scale fossil-fuel power stations in operation.

The vapour-compression refrigeration cycle is used in almost all household refrigerators, air conditioners and heat pumps. A refrigerant vapour is compressed, condensed to a liquid while rejecting heat to the warm surroundings, passed through an expansion valve where its pressure and temperature drop sharply in a throttling process, and evaporated in the cold space while absorbing heat. The choice of refrigerant involves trade-offs between thermodynamic performance, safety and environmental impact. Chlorofluorocarbons, once widely used, were phased out under the Montreal Protocol of 1987 because they damage the stratospheric ozone layer, and many of their replacements are being phased down because they are powerful greenhouse gases.

Heat pumps are attracting attention as a way to heat buildings with less energy than direct electric heating. Because a heat pump moves heat rather than generating it, it can deliver several joules of heat for each joule of electricity it consumes. The coefficient of performance falls as the temperature difference between the outdoor air and the indoor space grows, so heat pumps work best in mild climates or with ground-source heat exchangers, which draw heat from the relatively constant temperature of the ground a few metres below the surface.

Chapter 8. Kinetic Theory of Gases

Kinetic theory connects the bulk properties of a gas to the motion of its molecules. It starts from a simple model: a gas consists of a very large number of identical molecules in constant random motion, the molecules are small compared with the distances between them, they collide elastically with each other and with the walls, and between collisions they move in straight lines at constant speed. From these assumptions kinetic theory derives the ideal gas law and gives a physical meaning to temperature.

The pressure of a gas arises from molecular collisions with the walls of its container. Each time a molecule strikes a wall and rebounds, its momentum perpendicular to the wall reverses, and the wall must supply the force that produces this change. Summing over all the molecules striking a wall per second gives a steady average force, and dividing by the area of the wall gives the pressure. The calculation shows that the pressure equals one third of the number density of molecules multiplied by the mass of one molecule and by the mean of the squared molecular speed.

Comparing this result with the ideal gas law leads to one of the central results of kinetic theory: the average translational kinetic energy of a molecule is three halves of kT, where k is the Boltzmann constant, about 1.381 times 10 to the power minus 23 joules per kelvin. Temperature is therefore a measure of the average kinetic energy of molecular translation. Two gases at the same temperature have the same average molecular kinetic energy, even if their molecules have very different masses; the lighter molecules simply move faster.

The root-mean-square speed of the molecules, the square root of the mean squared speed, equals the square root of 3kT divided by the molecular mass. For nitrogen at room temperature this speed is about 500 metres per second, comparable to the speed of sound in air, which is not a coincidence, since sound is carried by molecular motion. Hydrogen molecules, which are fourteen times lighter than nitrogen molecules, move about 3.7 times faster at the same temperature.

The molecules of a gas do not all move at the same speed. Their speeds follow the Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution, which rises from zero at zero speed to a maximum at the most probable speed and then falls off with a long tail towards high speeds. As the temperature increases, the peak of the distribution moves to higher speeds and the distribution becomes broader and flatter. The high-speed tail is important for chemistry, because only molecules with enough energy can react, and a modest rise in temperature greatly increases the fraction of molecules in the tail.

Three characteristic speeds describe the Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution: the most probable speed, the mean speed and the root-mean-square speed. They are in the ratio of about 1 to 1.13 to 1.22, so the most probable speed is the smallest and the root-mean-square speed the largest. For most purposes the distinction matters little, but it must be kept in mind when comparing results from different textbooks.

A molecule in a gas travels, on average, a certain distance between collisions, called the mean free path. The mean free path is inversely proportional to the number density of molecules and to the square of the molecular diameter. For air at atmospheric pressure and room temperature the mean free path is about 70 nanometres, a few hundred times the diameter of a molecule. At the very low pressures used in vacuum systems the mean free path can exceed the size of the apparatus, and the molecules then collide with the walls far more often than with each other.

Kinetic theory also explains the transport properties of gases. Viscosity arises because molecules moving between layers of gas flowing at different speeds carry momentum from one layer to another. Thermal conductivity arises because molecules carry kinetic energy from hotter regions to colder ones. Diffusion arises because molecules wander randomly through the gas. A surprising prediction of kinetic theory, confirmed by Maxwell's own experiments, is that the viscosity of a gas does not depend on its pressure over a wide range, because a lower density of molecules is exactly compensated by a longer mean free path.

Effusion is the escape of gas through a hole small compared with the mean free path. Because lighter molecules move faster, they escape more quickly, and the rate of effusion is inversely proportional to the square root of the molecular mass, a result known as Graham's law. Effusion through porous barriers was used on a large industrial scale to separate the isotopes of uranium, using the gas uranium hexafluoride, because molecules containing the lighter isotope uranium-235 effuse slightly faster than those containing uranium-238.

Brownian motion provided some of the most direct evidence for the existence of molecules. In 1827 the botanist Robert Brown observed through a microscope that pollen grains suspended in water move in an irregular, jittery way. In 1905 Albert Einstein explained this motion as the result of unbalanced impacts of water molecules on the grains and predicted how far a grain should wander in a given time. Jean Perrin confirmed Einstein's predictions experimentally a few years later and used them to measure the Avogadro constant, which convinced the remaining sceptics that atoms and molecules are real.

Chapter 9. Mixtures, Humidity and Air Conditioning

Many practical problems involve mixtures of gases rather than pure substances. Dalton's law of partial pressures states that the total pressure of a mixture of ideal gases equals the sum of the partial pressures of its components, where the partial pressure of a component is the pressure it would exert if it alone occupied the whole volume at the same temperature. The partial pressure of each component is proportional to its mole fraction, so in dry air, which is about 78 percent nitrogen and 21 percent oxygen by volume, the partial pressure of oxygen at sea level is about 21 kilopascals.

Mixing two different gases is an irreversible process that increases entropy, even when the gases are ideal and no heat is exchanged. The entropy of mixing arises because each gas expands from its original volume into the whole volume of the container. When the gases are identical, there is no increase in entropy on removing a partition between them, because nothing distinguishable has changed. This observation, known as the Gibbs paradox, puzzled physicists until quantum mechanics explained that identical particles are truly indistinguishable.

Atmospheric air always contains some water vapour, and its amount has a large effect on comfort, on the weather and on the performance of machinery. The study of moist air is called psychrometrics. The absolute humidity, or humidity ratio, is the mass of water vapour per unit mass of dry air. The relative humidity is the ratio of the partial pressure of water vapour in the air to the saturation vapour pressure at the same temperature, usually expressed as a percentage. Because the saturation vapour pressure rises steeply with temperature, warming air without adding water lowers its relative humidity, which is why heated buildings feel dry in winter.

The dew point is the temperature to which air must be cooled, at constant pressure and constant water content, for condensation to begin. When a cold drink is taken from a refrigerator on a humid day, the air in contact with the glass cools below its dew point and water condenses on the outside of the glass. Clouds and fog form in the same way, when moist air is cooled below its dew point by rising and expanding, or by passing over cold ground.

The wet-bulb temperature is measured by a thermometer whose bulb is covered with a wet wick and exposed to moving air. Evaporation from the wick cools the bulb, and the drier the air, the faster the evaporation and the larger the cooling. The difference between the dry-bulb and wet-bulb temperatures therefore indicates the humidity, and the instrument that measures both, called a psychrometer, is the traditional method of measuring humidity. The wet-bulb temperature is also important for human health, because sweating cools the body by evaporation, and when the wet-bulb temperature approaches body temperature the body can no longer shed heat.

The psychrometric chart summarises the properties of moist air at a given total pressure. It plots the humidity ratio against the dry-bulb temperature and shows lines of constant relative humidity, wet-bulb temperature, specific enthalpy and specific volume. Air-conditioning engineers use the chart to design systems that move air from one state to another. Cooling and dehumidification, for example, is carried out by passing warm, humid air over a coil colder than its dew point, so that water condenses on the coil and the air leaves cooler and drier.

Evaporative coolers, sometimes called swamp coolers, cool air by passing it over wet pads so that water evaporates into it. The process takes heat from the air and supplies it to the evaporating water, lowering the air temperature while raising its humidity. Evaporative coolers use much less energy than vapour-compression air conditioners, but they work well only in dry climates, because in humid air little additional water can evaporate.

Cooling towers at power stations and large buildings work on a similar principle. Warm water from a condenser is sprayed into a stream of air, a small fraction of it evaporates, and the evaporation cools the remaining water, which is collected and returned to the condenser. The white plumes seen above cooling towers consist of water droplets formed when the warm, saturated air leaving the tower mixes with cooler outside air, not of smoke or pollutants.

Chapter 10. Laboratory Methods and Measurement

Accurate thermodynamic measurements require careful control of heat leaks, temperature gradients and calibration errors. This chapter describes the instruments used in the course laboratory and the procedures students must follow.

Temperatures in the laboratory are measured with three kinds of sensor. Liquid-in-glass thermometers are simple and need no power, but they are fragile and slow to respond. Thermocouples, which produce a small voltage at the junction of two different metals that depends on the temperature, are robust and respond quickly but require a reference junction and careful calibration. Platinum resistance thermometers, whose electrical resistance rises nearly linearly with temperature, are the most accurate and stable and serve as the laboratory standard. Every sensor used for a graded experiment must be checked against the platinum resistance thermometer at the start of the session.

The calorimetry experiments in the course follow a common procedure. The calorimeter is filled with a measured mass of water and left until its temperature is steady. A sample of known mass, heated in a water bath to a known temperature, is transferred quickly into the calorimeter, and the temperature is recorded every fifteen seconds until it reaches a maximum and begins to fall. The maximum temperature is not used directly. Instead, the cooling curve after the maximum is extrapolated back to the moment of transfer, which corrects for the heat lost to the surroundings during mixing.

For the energy balance in Experiment 3, students must include three terms: the heat lost by the sample, the heat gained by the water, and the heat gained by the calorimeter vessel. The aluminium calorimeter vessel used in Experiment 3 has a heat capacity of 120 J/K, and reports that omit the vessel or use a different value will lose marks for the analysis section. Students who obtain a specific heat capacity for the sample that differs from the reference value by more than ten percent are expected to discuss the likely sources of error.

The gas law experiments use a sealed syringe connected to a pressure sensor and immersed in a temperature-controlled water bath. By recording pressure at several volumes at constant temperature, students verify Boyle's law, and by recording pressure at several temperatures at constant volume, they extrapolate to the temperature at which the pressure of an ideal gas would fall to zero. A careful experiment gives a value for absolute zero within a few kelvins of minus 273 degrees Celsius.

Every experiment requires a written uncertainty analysis. Students must identify the main sources of uncertainty, estimate the uncertainty of each measured quantity, and combine them to find the uncertainty of the final result. When independent uncertainties are combined in a sum, their squares are added and the square root of the total is taken. A result reported without an uncertainty, or with an uncertainty that is clearly too small, is treated as incomplete.

Safety rules in the laboratory are strict. Hot water baths and heating plates can cause burns, and glass apparatus can shatter when heated unevenly. Students must wear safety glasses at all times, must never leave a heating plate switched on while unattended, and must report any breakage to the demonstrator immediately. Liquid nitrogen, used in the low-temperature demonstration, may only be handled by staff.

Chapter 12. The Third Law and Low Temperatures

The third law of thermodynamics concerns the behaviour of entropy as the temperature approaches absolute zero. In the form given by Walther Nernst, it states that the entropy change of any process between equilibrium states approaches zero as the temperature approaches absolute zero. In the form usually given by Max Planck, it states that the entropy of a perfect crystal of a pure substance is zero at absolute zero. The Planck statement provides a natural reference point, so that entropies can be given absolute values rather than only differences.

An important consequence of the third law is that absolute zero cannot be reached in any finite number of steps. Each step of a cooling process, such as an adiabatic expansion or a demagnetisation, lowers the temperature by a smaller amount as the temperature falls, because the entropy differences that the process exploits shrink towards zero. Laboratories have reached temperatures of a small fraction of a microkelvin, but never absolute zero itself.

Heat capacities also vanish as the temperature approaches absolute zero. For non-metallic crystals at low temperatures, the heat capacity is proportional to the cube of the absolute temperature, a result known as the Debye T-cubed law. In metals there is an additional contribution from the conduction electrons that is proportional to the temperature itself, and at the lowest temperatures this electronic term dominates.

The third law in its Planck form applies only to perfect crystals in true equilibrium. Some substances retain a residual entropy at absolute zero because their molecules are frozen into a disordered arrangement before they can reach the ordered state. Carbon monoxide is the classic example: each linear CO molecule can sit in the crystal in one of two nearly equivalent orientations, and the orientations become frozen in at random as the crystal cools. The measured residual entropy of solid carbon monoxide is about 4.6 J/(mol·K), somewhat less than the value of R ln 2, about 5.8 J/(mol·K), that complete orientational disorder would give. Ordinary ice also has a residual entropy, because the hydrogen atoms in its crystal can be arranged in many different ways consistent with the structure; Linus Pauling estimated this residual entropy in 1935 by counting the allowed hydrogen arrangements.

Low-temperature physics has revealed phenomena that have no counterpart at ordinary temperatures. Helium-4 becomes a superfluid below about 2.17 K, flowing without viscosity and climbing the walls of its container as a thin film. Many metals and alloys become superconductors at low temperatures, losing all electrical resistance. These effects are macroscopic manifestations of quantum mechanics, and they are among the reasons that the study of thermodynamics at very low temperatures remains an active field of research.

Summary of the Reader

This reader has developed classical thermodynamics from the concepts of system, state and equilibrium through the first law, which expresses the conservation of energy, the second law, which introduces entropy and limits the efficiency of engines, and the third law, which fixes the behaviour of entropy at absolute zero. Students should be able to apply the first law to closed and steady-flow systems, compute entropy changes for simple processes, explain the Carnot limit and its practical consequences, and use the Gibbs free energy to decide whether a process can occur spontaneously. The review questions in Chapter 11 test the material of the first nine chapters and some topics that will be taught in the second half of the course.
